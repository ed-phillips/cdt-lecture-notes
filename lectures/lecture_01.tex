\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{modality} is ``the way in which something happens or is
        experienced'', and a research problem is multimodal when it includes
        several such modalities~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. Examples include
        text, images, sound, waveforms and tables.
  \item \emph{Multimodal machine learning} aims to build models that can process
        and relate information from multiple modalities~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}.
  \item Liang et al.~\cite{liang2024foundations} describe multimodal data through
        three principles: \emph{heterogeneity}, \emph{connections} and
        \emph{interactions}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Healthcare.} Medicine is inherently multimodal, drawing on biobanks,
health records, imaging, biosensors, and genomic and microbiome data. Applications
range from discovery and diagnosis to prognosis, treatment and prevention, and
include personalised medicine, remote monitoring, digital clinical trials and
digital twins~\cite{acosta2022multimodal}.

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Baltru\v{s}aitis, Ahuja \& Morency~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}: the
        standard survey and taxonomy.
  \item Acosta et al.~\cite{acosta2022multimodal}: opportunities and challenges of
        multimodal AI in medicine.
  \item Liang, Zadeh \& Morency~\cite{liang2024foundations}: a more recent survey.
\end{furtherreading}


\section{A brief history}
\label{sec:history}

Some examples of how multimodality has been studied over time:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Audio-visual speech.} In the McGurk effect~\cite{mcgurk1976hearing},
        hearing ``ba'' while watching lips say ``ga'' is perceived as ``da''.
        Audio-visual speech recognition was one of the earliest areas of
        multimodal machine learning~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}.
  \item \textbf{1990s and 2000s: multimedia.} Indexing and retrieval of images and
        video by their content, beyond keyword search.
  \item \textbf{2000s: human social interaction.} For example, the AMI Meeting
        Corpus~\cite{mccowan2005ami}: around 100 hours of meetings recorded with
        microphones, cameras and pens.
  \item \textbf{2010s: deep learning, mostly text and images.} Ngiam et
        al.~\cite{ngiam2011multimodal} learnt shared representations of audio and
        video; image and text models followed (Section~\ref{sec:llms}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Baltru\v{s}aitis et al.~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}, Section~2.
\end{furtherreading}

\section{Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

\paragraph{Why fuse?} To make predictions more robust when a modality is missing
or of poor quality, and to use complementary information across modalities.
Clinical studies that compare multimodal with unimodal models mostly report
gains~\cite{kline2022multimodal}, but how much depends on the data and the
task~\cite{krones2025review}.

\paragraph{Where to fuse.} Fusion is usually described as early, intermediate (or
joint) or late, depending on where the modalities are combined
(Figure~\ref{fig:fusion_paradigms})~\cite{huang2020fusion,krones2025review}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/fusion_paradigms}
  \caption{Early, intermediate and late fusion, illustrated with an EHR and an
           ECG. After Huang et al.~\cite{huang2020fusion}.}
  \label{fig:fusion_paradigms}
\end{figure}

Write $\mathbf{x}_{E}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{C}$ for the inputs from two modalities
(here the EHR and the ECG), and $\hat{y}$ for the prediction.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Early fusion} joins the input features, or features extracted from
        each modality, and trains one model $f$:
        \begin{equation}
          \hat{y} = f\left([\mathbf{x}_{E}; \mathbf{x}_{C}]\right),
        \end{equation}
        where $[\cdot\,;\cdot]$ denotes concatenation.
  \item \textbf{Intermediate fusion} learns an encoder $\phi_m$ for each modality,
        combines the representations $\mathbf{h}_m = \phi_m(\mathbf{x}_m)$ with an
        operator $c$, and trains a head $g$, all end to end:
        \begin{equation}
          \hat{y} = g\left(c(\mathbf{h}_{E}, \mathbf{h}_{C})\right).
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Late fusion} trains a model $f_m$ on each modality separately and
        combines their predictions with a function $\psi$:
        \begin{equation}
          \hat{y} = \psi\left(f_{E}(\mathbf{x}_{E}), f_{C}(\mathbf{x}_{C})\right),
        \end{equation}
        for example a weighted average of predicted probabilities,
        $\hat{p} = w\,\hat{p}_{E} + (1 - w)\,\hat{p}_{C}$, an average of log-odds,
        or a small model trained on the outputs (stacking).
\end{itemize}
Krones et al.~\cite{krones2025review} also review mixed strategies, and the
features each kind of fusion operates on. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2020fusion}
give implementation guidelines.

\paragraph{How to combine.} Common choices for the operator $c$ are shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:fusion_operators}. With $\mathbf{h}_{E}, \mathbf{h}_{C} \in
\mathbb{R}^{d}$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Concatenation} keeps both representations side by side, doubling
        the size:
        \begin{equation}
          \mathbf{z} = [\mathbf{h}_{E}; \mathbf{h}_{C}] \in \mathbb{R}^{2d}.
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Addition} keeps the size fixed, usually after projecting each
        modality with a learnt matrix:
        \begin{equation}
          \mathbf{z} = W_{E}\mathbf{h}_{E} + W_{C}\mathbf{h}_{C}.
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Gating} learns how much to take from each modality, per feature and
        per example. In the gated multimodal unit of Arevalo et
        al.~\cite{arevalo2017gated},
        \begin{equation}
          \begin{gathered}
            \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_m = \tanh(W_m \mathbf{x}_m), \qquad
            \mathbf{g} = \sigma\left(W_g [\mathbf{x}_{E}; \mathbf{x}_{C}]\right), \\
            \mathbf{z} = \mathbf{g} \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{E} + (1 - \mathbf{g}) \odot \tilde{\mathbf{h}}_{C},
          \end{gathered}
        \end{equation}
        where $\sigma$ is the logistic sigmoid and $\odot$ the element-wise product.
  \item \textbf{Feature-wise modulation} (FiLM~\cite{perez2018film}) lets one
        modality scale and shift the features of another:
        \begin{equation}
          \mathbf{z} = \symbf{\gamma}(\mathbf{x}_{E}) \odot \mathbf{h}_{C} + \symbf{\beta}(\mathbf{x}_{E}),
        \end{equation}
        with $\symbf{\gamma}$ and $\symbf{\beta}$ learnt functions.
  \item \textbf{Tensor fusion}~\cite{zadeh2017tensor} takes the outer product of the
        representations, each extended with a 1, so that $\mathbf{z}$ contains the
        unimodal features and every pairwise product between them:
        \begin{equation}
          \mathbf{z} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{E} \\ 1\end{bmatrix} \otimes
                       \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{h}_{C} \\ 1\end{bmatrix}
                     \in \mathbb{R}^{(d+1)^2}.
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Attention over modalities} weights each modality by a score computed
        from the inputs. With a learnt query $\mathbf{q}$ and keys
        $\mathbf{k}_m = W_K \mathbf{h}_m$,
        \begin{equation}
          \alpha_m = \frac{\exp\left(\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{k}_m / \sqrt{d}\right)}
                          {\sum_{m'} \exp\left(\mathbf{q}^\top \mathbf{k}_{m'} / \sqrt{d}\right)},
          \qquad
          \mathbf{z} = \sum_m \alpha_m \mathbf{h}_m.
        \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{A learnt shared space} trains the encoders so that matching inputs
        from the two modalities have similar representations (see CLIP,
        Section~\ref{sec:clip}). The representations can then be compared or
        combined directly.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/fusion_operators}
  \caption{Ways to combine an EHR representation (orange) and an ECG
           representation (blue) into a fused representation (green).}
  \label{fig:fusion_operators}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Fusion in transformers.} Transformers are built on scaled dot-product
attention. For queries $Q$, keys $K$ and values $V$ (each a matrix with one row
per token),
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Attn}(Q, K, V) = \operatorname{softmax}\left(\frac{Q K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V.
\end{equation}
Xu, Zhu \& Clifton~\cite{xu2023multimodal} survey how this is used to fuse
modalities. With token sequences $X_A$ and $X_B$ from two modalities:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Early summation or concatenation} feeds $X_A + X_B$ (for aligned
        tokens) or $[X_A; X_B]$ to one transformer, so self-attention runs across
        both modalities.
  \item \textbf{Hierarchical} designs encode each modality with its own
        transformer layers, then fuse the outputs with further layers.
  \item \textbf{Cross-attention} takes the queries from one modality and the keys
        and values from the other:
        \begin{equation}
          Z_A = \operatorname{Attn}\left(X_A W_Q,\; X_B W_K,\; X_B W_V\right),
        \end{equation}
        so each token of $A$ gathers information from $B$. The Multimodal
        Transformer~\cite{tsai2019multimodal} uses directional cross-attention of
        this kind to fuse time series sampled at different rates, without
        aligning them first.
  \item \textbf{Attention bottlenecks}~\cite{nagrani2021attention} force all
        exchange between modalities through a few shared tokens
        $Z_{\text{fsn}}$. At each layer $l$, each modality $i$ attends over its own
        tokens and the bottleneck, and the bottleneck updates are averaged:
        \begin{equation}
          \begin{gathered}
            \left[Z_i^{l+1} \,\|\, \hat{Z}_{\text{fsn},i}^{l+1}\right]
              = \operatorname{Transformer}\left(\left[Z_i^{l} \,\|\, Z_{\text{fsn}}^{l}\right]; \theta_i\right), \\
            Z_{\text{fsn}}^{l+1} = \operatorname{Avg}_i\, \hat{Z}_{\text{fsn},i}^{l+1}.
          \end{gathered}
        \end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Cautions from the literature.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Multimodal networks can underperform the best unimodal one, because they
        overfit more and their modalities learn at different
        rates~\cite{wang2020what,huang2022modality}.
  \item Models can be sensitive to missing modalities~\cite{ma2022multimodal}.
  \item In a recent EHR and chest X-ray benchmark, fusion helped most when both
        modalities were present, and the benefit fell quickly as data went
        missing~\cite{yin2026when}.
\end{itemize}

\section{EHRs as multimodal timelines}
\label{sec:ehr}

Longitudinal EHRs combine many data types that evolve over time, including
diagnosis codes, laboratory results, medications, notes, imaging and
signals~\cite{fries2025foundation}.

\paragraph{Next event instead of next token.} ETHOS~\cite{renc2024zero} turns each
patient's record into a sequence of tokens $e_1, e_2, \dots, e_T$: medical events,
with tokens for the time intervals between them. A transformer is trained to
predict the next token, as a language model is:
\begin{equation}
  p_\theta(e_1, \dots, e_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta(e_t \mid e_{<t}).
\end{equation}
Outcomes are then predicted zero-shot by simulation: starting from a patient's
history, generate $R$ possible future timelines and count the $M$ that contain
the outcome (for example death before discharge), giving $\hat{p} = M / R$.
The paper uses $R = 20$.

\paragraph{Time to event.} MOTOR~\cite{steinberg2024motor} predicts how long until
each of many future events (medical codes), rather than only the next event.
It uses a piecewise exponential model: time is split into pieces
$[S_p, E_p)$, and for event type $k$ the hazard is constant within each piece,
$\lambda_{kp}$. The probability of not having had event $k$ by time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  S_k(t) = \prod_{p} \exp\left(-\lambda_{kp} \, \max\left(0,\, \min(t, E_p) - S_p\right)\right).
\end{equation}
Training maximises the likelihood $\lambda^{\delta} \exp(-\lambda U)$ in each
piece, where $U$ is the time spent in the piece and $\delta$ indicates whether
the event happened there. To scale to thousands of event types, the log hazard
is factorised as $\log \lambda_{kp} = \mathbf{m}_p^\top \symbf{\beta}_k$,
with $\mathbf{m}_p$ derived from the transformer's representation of the patient
and $\symbf{\beta}_k$ an embedding of the event type. MOTOR was pretrained
on up to 55 million patient records.

\paragraph{Multimodal patient journeys.} Kim et al.~\cite{kim2026computable}
combine structured EHR data with information extracted from clinical notes by
language models. NOAH~\cite{susetzky2026noah} models full patient journeys across
the MIMIC datasets, including images, time series and structured and
unstructured records. It encodes each modality with its own encoder and models
the next event $x_t$ through a latent patient state $\mathbf{z}_t$:
\begin{equation}
  p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) = \int p_\theta(x_t \mid \mathbf{z}_t)\, p_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t \mid \mathbf{h}_{<t})\, d\mathbf{z}_t,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{h}_{<t}$ summarises the history, and is trained variationally,
with prior and posterior networks for $\mathbf{z}_t$.

\section{LLMs as multimodal backbones}
\label{sec:llms}

\paragraph{Projecting into the language model.} LLaVA~\cite{liu2023visual}
connects an existing image encoder $g$ to an LLM through a learnt projection $W$,
which maps the image features into the language model's token-embedding space:
\begin{equation}
  H_v = W Z_v, \qquad Z_v = g(X_v).
\end{equation}
The projected tokens $H_v$ are placed in the LLM's input alongside the text, and
the model is trained to generate the answer $X_a$ to an instruction
$X_{\text{instruct}}$ one token at a time:
\begin{equation}
  p(X_a \mid X_v, X_{\text{instruct}}) = \prod_{i=1}^{L} p_\theta\left(x_i \mid X_v, X_{\text{instruct},<i}, X_{a,<i}\right).
\end{equation}

Flamingo~\cite{alayrac2022flamingo} instead keeps the LLM frozen and inserts new
cross-attention layers between its layers, with queries from the text and keys and
values from the visual features. Each new layer's output is scaled by a learnt
gate before it joins the residual stream,
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y} \leftarrow \mathbf{y} + \tanh(\alpha)\, \operatorname{XAttn}(\mathbf{y}, X_v),
\end{equation}
with $\alpha$ initialised at 0, so that training starts from the unchanged
language model. A ``Perceiver Resampler'' first reduces any number of visual
features to a fixed set of 64 tokens.

\paragraph{Aligning representations: CLIP.}
\label{sec:clip}
CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} learns a shared image and text space from 400
million image--caption pairs. For a batch of $N$ pairs with normalised embeddings
$\mathbf{e}_i$ and $\mathbf{t}_j$ and temperature $\tau$, let
$s_{ij} = \mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{t}_j / \tau$. The loss for matching images to
captions is
\begin{equation}
  L_{\text{image}\to\text{text}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \log \frac{\exp(s_{ii})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(s_{ij})},
\end{equation}
and CLIP averages it with the same loss over the columns:
\begin{equation}
  L = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(L_{\text{image}\to\text{text}} + L_{\text{text}\to\text{image}}\right).
\end{equation}
This is the InfoNCE loss~\cite{vandenoord2018representation}, applied in both
directions. The aligned space enables zero-shot classification: embed a text
prompt $\mathbf{t}_c$ for each class $c$ (such as ``a photo of a dog''), and assign
an image to the class whose prompt is most similar,
$\hat{c} = \arg\max_c \, \mathbf{e} \cdot \mathbf{t}_c$. MERL~\cite{liu2024zeroshot} applies
the same idea to ECGs and their reports, and ImageBind~\cite{girdhar2023imagebind}
extends it to six modalities.

\paragraph{Medical examples.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item HeLM~\cite{belyaeva2023multimodal} serialises tabular data as text and
        maps complex modalities, such as spirograms, into the LLM's

\section{The LLM context as unifier}
\label{sec:unifier}

Representation learning, alignment and fusion require data and effort, while
LLMs are flexible in their input. Ji et al.~\cite{ji2026portable} use a frozen LLM
to summarise each patient's irregular ICU time series $\mathbf{x}$ as text, then
embed the summary with a frozen text-embedding model $E$, giving a
representation $\mathbf{z} = E\left(\operatorname{LLM}(\operatorname{prompt}(\mathbf{x}))\right)$
for downstream predictors. These
representations were competitive with imputation, self-supervised learning and
time series foundation models, and transferred better to new hospitals.

Agents equipped with tools offer a further alternative; see
Chapter~\ref{chap:lecture_02}.
