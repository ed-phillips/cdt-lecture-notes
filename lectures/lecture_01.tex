\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A \emph{modality} is ``the way in which something happens or is
        experienced'', and a research problem is multimodal when it includes
        several such modalities~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. Examples include
        text, images, sound, waveforms and tables.
  \item \emph{Multimodal machine learning} aims to build models that can process
        and relate information from multiple modalities~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}.
  \item Liang et al.~\cite{liang2024foundations} describe multimodal data through
        three principles: \emph{heterogeneity}, \emph{connections} and
        \emph{interactions}.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Healthcare.} Medicine is inherently multimodal, drawing on biobanks,
health records, imaging, biosensors, and genomic and microbiome data. Applications
range from discovery and diagnosis to prognosis, treatment and prevention, and
include personalised medicine, remote monitoring, digital clinical trials and
digital twins~\cite{acosta2022multimodal}.

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Baltru\v{s}aitis, Ahuja \& Morency~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}: the
        standard survey and taxonomy.
  \item Acosta et al.~\cite{acosta2022multimodal}: opportunities and challenges of
        multimodal AI in medicine.
  \item Liang, Zadeh \& Morency~\cite{liang2024foundations}: a more recent survey.
\end{furtherreading}


\section{A brief history}
\label{sec:history}

Some examples of how multimodality has been studied over time:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Audio-visual speech.} In the McGurk effect~\cite{mcgurk1976hearing},
        hearing ``ba'' while watching lips say ``ga'' is perceived as ``da''.
        Audio-visual speech recognition was one of the earliest areas of
        multimodal machine learning~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}.
  \item \textbf{1990s and 2000s: multimedia.} Indexing and retrieval of images and
        video by their content, beyond keyword search.
  \item \textbf{2000s: human social interaction.} For example, the AMI Meeting
        Corpus~\cite{mccowan2005ami}: around 100 hours of meetings recorded with
        microphones, cameras and pens.
  \item \textbf{2010s: deep learning, mostly text and images.} Ngiam et
        al.~\cite{ngiam2011multimodal} learnt shared representations of audio and
        video; image and text models followed (Section~\ref{sec:llms}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Baltru\v{s}aitis et al.~\cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}, Section~2.
\end{furtherreading}

\section{Fusion}
\label{sec:fusion}

\paragraph{Why fuse?} To make predictions more robust when a modality is missing
or of poor quality, and to use complementary information across modalities.
Clinical studies that compare multimodal with unimodal models mostly report
gains~\cite{kline2022multimodal}, but how much depends on the data and the
task~\cite{krones2025review}.

\paragraph{Where to fuse.} Fusion is usually described as early, intermediate (or
joint) or late, depending on where the modalities are combined
(Figure~\ref{fig:fusion_paradigms})~\cite{huang2020fusion,krones2025review}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/fusion_paradigms}
  \caption{Early, intermediate and late fusion, illustrated with an EHR and an
           ECG. After Huang et al.~\cite{huang2020fusion}.}
  \label{fig:fusion_paradigms}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Early:} join the input features and train one model.
  \item \textbf{Intermediate:} learn a representation of each modality, combine the
        representations, and train end to end.
  \item \textbf{Late:} train one model per modality and combine their predictions.
\end{itemize}
Krones et al.~\cite{krones2025review} also review mixed strategies, and the
features each kind of fusion operates on. Huang et al.~\cite{huang2020fusion}
give implementation guidelines.

\paragraph{How to combine.} Common operations for combining two representations
are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fusion_operators}: concatenation, addition, gating
(e.g.\ gated multimodal units~\cite{arevalo2017gated}), attention, and learning
a shared space. Others include feature-wise modulation (FiLM~\cite{perez2018film})
and outer products (tensor fusion~\cite{zadeh2017tensor}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{generated/fusion_operators}
  \caption{Ways to combine an EHR representation (orange) and an ECG
           representation (blue) into a fused representation (green).}
  \label{fig:fusion_operators}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Fusion in transformers.} Xu, Zhu \& Clifton~\cite{xu2023multimodal}
survey how transformers fuse modalities: early summation or concatenation of the
token sequences, hierarchical attention, and cross-attention between modalities.
Examples include cross-modal attention over unaligned time series~\cite{tsai2019multimodal}
and attention bottlenecks~\cite{nagrani2021attention}.

\paragraph{Cautions from the literature.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Multimodal networks can underperform the best unimodal one, because they
        overfit more and their modalities learn at different
        rates~\cite{wang2020what,huang2022modality}.
  \item Models can be sensitive to missing modalities~\cite{ma2022multimodal}.
  \item In a recent EHR and chest X-ray benchmark, fusion helped most when both
        modalities were present, and the benefit fell quickly as data went
        missing~\cite{yin2026when}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Huang et al.~\cite{huang2020fusion} and Krones et al.~\cite{krones2025review}:
        fusion in healthcare.
  \item Xu, Zhu \& Clifton~\cite{xu2023multimodal}: multimodal transformers.
  \item Wu et al.~\cite{wu2026deep}: learning with missing modalities.
  \item Clinical examples: HAIM~\cite{soenksen2022integrated} and
        MedFuse~\cite{hayat2022medfuse}.
\end{furtherreading}


\section{EHRs as multimodal timelines}
\label{sec:ehr}

Longitudinal EHRs combine many data types that evolve over time, including
diagnosis codes, laboratory results, medications, notes, imaging and
signals~\cite{fries2025foundation}.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Next event instead of next token.} ETHOS~\cite{renc2024zero} treats
        medical events as tokens and trains a transformer on patient timelines to
        predict future health trajectories zero-shot, without task-specific
        labels or fine-tuning.
  \item \textbf{Time to event.} MOTOR~\cite{steinberg2024motor} predicts the
        distribution of times to many future events rather than only the next
        one. It was pretrained on up to 55 million patient records.
  \item \textbf{Multimodal patient journeys.} Kim et al.~\cite{kim2026computable}
        combine structured EHR data with information extracted from clinical
        notes by language models. NOAH~\cite{susetzky2026noah} models full patient
        journeys across the MIMIC datasets, including images, time series and
        structured and unstructured records.
\end{itemize}

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Fries~\cite{fries2025foundation}: lecture slides on foundation models for EHRs.
  \item Wornow et al.~\cite{wornow2023shaky}: a review of EHR foundation models and
        how to evaluate them; EHRSHOT~\cite{wornow2023ehrshot} is a benchmark.
  \item Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2023improving}: irregular ICU time series and notes.
\end{furtherreading}


\section{Example: ECGs and EHRs}
\label{sec:ecg_ehr}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Deep learning on the 12-lead ECG alone predicts one-year mortality, including
        for ECGs read as normal~\cite{raghunath2020prediction}.
  \item A single ECG model can predict hundreds of diagnosis codes from the linked
        EHR~\cite{strodthoff2024prospects}.
  \item Khurshid et al.~\cite{khurshid2022ecg} combined a deep learning model of the
        ECG with a clinical risk score (CHARGE-AF) to predict atrial fibrillation.
        The combination performed best in all three cohorts studied, and the two
        were complementary.
\end{itemize}

\section{LLMs as multimodal backbones}
\label{sec:llms}

\paragraph{Projecting into the language model.} LLaVA~\cite{liu2023visual} connects
an existing image encoder to an LLM through a learnt projection, aligning the
image representations with the language model's embedding space, and then tunes
on visual instructions. Flamingo~\cite{alayrac2022flamingo} instead adds
cross-attention layers to a frozen LLM.

\paragraph{Aligning representations: CLIP.}
\label{sec:clip}
CLIP~\cite{radford2021learning} learns a shared image and text space from 400
million image--caption pairs. For a batch of $N$ pairs with normalised embeddings
$\mathbf{e}_i$ and $\mathbf{t}_j$ and temperature $\tau$, let
$s_{ij} = \mathbf{e}_i \cdot \mathbf{t}_j / \tau$. The loss for matching images to
captions is
\begin{equation}
  L_{\text{image}\to\text{text}} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}
    \log \frac{\exp(s_{ii})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N} \exp(s_{ij})},
\end{equation}
and CLIP averages it with the same loss in the other direction. This is the
InfoNCE loss~\cite{vandenoord2018representation}. The aligned space enables
zero-shot classification from text prompts. MERL~\cite{liu2024zeroshot} applies
the same idea to ECGs and their reports, and ImageBind~\cite{girdhar2023imagebind}
extends it to six modalities.

\paragraph{Medical examples.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item HeLM~\cite{belyaeva2023multimodal} serialises tabular data as text and
        encodes complex modalities, such as spirograms, into the LLM's embedding
        space.
  \item Med-PaLM M~\cite{tu2024towards} handles clinical language, imaging and
        genomics with one set of model weights.
\end{itemize}

\begin{furtherreading}
  \item Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2024large} and Liu et al.~\cite{liu2025towards}:
        surveys of LLMs and cross-modal models for time series.
  \item Wearable data and language: Health-LLM~\cite{kim2024healthllm},
        PH-LLM~\cite{belyaeva2025personal} and SensorLM~\cite{zhang2025sensorlm}.
  \item ECG multimodal LLMs: GEM~\cite{lan2025gem} and PULSE~\cite{liu2024teach}.
\end{furtherreading}


\section{The LLM context as unifier}
\label{sec:unifier}

Representation learning, alignment and fusion require data and effort, while
LLMs are flexible in their input. Ji et al.~\cite{ji2026portable} use a frozen LLM
to summarise irregular ICU time series as text, then embed the summaries. These
representations were competitive with imputation, self-supervised learning and
time series foundation models, and transferred better to new hospitals.

Agents equipped with tools offer a further alternative; see
